## Strategy Still Has to Be Executed

Making the strategy is one job. Carrying it into the market is another, and most companies are weaker there. AI can draft a plan quickly, but execution still needs a human: you follow the plan, record what failed, and let that record change the next strategy.

A repeated story can become what people treat as true. *All Marketers Are Liars* uses a wine-glass seller: ordinary glasses at about **$1**, his glass at **$8 to $10**, sold on a curve that “improved” the wine, until a blind test found no difference. The hair-removal category and quick commerce grew the same way, by repeating an insecurity or a new habit until it felt like a fact.

The story still has to be believable, so a cream that whitens only one patch of one finger will not become a market just because you repeat it. Operating one ad platform is a skill. Choosing **where a customer will actually listen** is the strategy.

## What a Go-to-Market Plan Actually Answers

- **Official Definition:** A **go-to-market (GTM) plan** is the set of choices that take an offer from “we know who it is for” to “this is how we reach them, what we do there, and how we know it worked.”
- **In Simple Words:** It is the bridge between the positioning you already wrote and the money you are about to spend.
- **Real-Life Example:** FitFataak already knows the office professional and the 4 p.m. samosa. GTM asks which door that person actually walks through.

Five questions, in this order:

| Question | What you must write down |
|----------|--------------------------|
| **Who?** | The segment you are already pursuing |
| **What?** | The value proposition you are already taking to them |
| **Where?** | The channels that can reach that behaviour |
| **How?** | The campaigns, content, and sales motion |
| **How will we know?** | The few numbers that define success |

Read it as a chain: **audience → proposition → channel → activity → measurement**. Google, Instagram, LinkedIn, Snapchat, email, WhatsApp, influencers, and cold calling, all at once, is a shopping list. A friend to all is a friend to none.

![The go-to-market chain — who, what, where, how, and how we know](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session06/s06-02-gtm-chain.png?v=20261012)

## Do Not Start with the Channel

The weak opening question is “Should we run Meta ads?” The channel is a consequence. It is not the brief.

Work downwards:

1. **Who** is the customer?
2. **What behaviour** are you trying to change?
3. **Does demand already exist**, or do you have to create the feeling of a problem?
4. **Where** does this person already give attention?
5. **Which channel** matches that journey?
6. Only then do you book the platform.

Channel follows customer behaviour, not the marketer’s favourite app. If your buyer is 14 to 18, LinkedIn is the wrong room, because that room is full of working professionals. If the product needs a long explanation, a silent Instagram card will not carry it, because people there are looking, not reading an essay.

## Capture Demand and Generate Demand

Marketing does one of two jobs. You either **meet a search that already exists**, or you **create a reason to care** in someone who is not searching.

### Capture Demand

- **Official Definition:** **Demand capture** is marketing that intercepts people who are already looking for a solution.
- **In Simple Words:** Show up at the exact shelf where the hand is already reaching.
- **Real-Life Example:** Someone types “high protein snacks online.” FitFataak should be on that results page. The hunger was already named.

People look on **Google and Bing** (“leased line in NCR”), on **Amazon, Flipkart, and Myntra** (“t-shirt under 699,” sponsored rows first), and on Reddit, Quora, a store aisle, or a quick-commerce app. A **sponsored** result is a paid bid for that keyword, while **SEO** tries to earn the same shelf without a bid and takes longer. You do not open Instagram to hunt for a screen guard.

### Generate Demand

- **Official Definition:** **Demand generation** is marketing aimed at people who are not currently looking, so that they notice a problem, an offer, or both.
- **In Simple Words:** You walk into the room where they are already scrolling and you start the conversation.
- **Real-Life Example:** Millions of office workers never search “please save me from my 4 p.m. samosa.” They still live that moment. A reel of the samosa arriving at 4:30 p.m. can create the want. That is generation, not capture.

Generation uses filters: you describe who *might* buy, and the platform shows the ad while they do something else. A laptop brand can talk to people who just joined college, just got a job, or still use a three-year-old machine. Those are hypotheses, not proof they are shopping today.

The job, in order, is **awareness → interest → need**. The person may not know the problem has a name, or that your solution exists. The ad has to make both visible before a search box opens.

### In-Market and Not-in-Market

- **Official Definition:** An **in-market** buyer is actively shopping. A **not-in-market** buyer is living the problem, or could live it, but is not searching yet.
- **In Simple Words:** In-market people have their wallet half open. Everyone else has to be invited first.
- **Real-Life Example:** A class like this often starts **not-in-market**. A friend, a YouTube mention, or an Instagram reel creates the idea. The person then types the brand name, reads reviews, and only then pays. The reel **generated**. The search **captured**.

One path starts **in-market** (search, compare, buy), and the other starts **not-in-market** (see the story, feel the need, then search). Above roughly **₹5,000 to ₹10,000**, people rarely buy from the first ad alone. A **₹60** snack can, because the risk is small, so do not copy that “buy now” motion onto a cybersecurity contract.

## Search and Social Do Different Jobs

Neither channel is “better.” They answer different sentences.

| | **Search** | **Social** |
|--|------------|------------|
| Who speaks first | The customer: “I need something.” | The brand: “You might need this.” |
| The reply | “Here we are.” | “Hmm. Maybe.” |
| Intent | High. They typed a problem. | Passive. They were scrolling. |
| Strong at | Comparison, high-intent queries, existing demand | Discovery, desire, visual stories, repeated exposure |
| FitFataak job | Catch “high protein snacks online” | Show the 4:30 p.m. samosa moment |

Search says the brand showed up for a raised hand. Social says the brand raised the hand first. FitFataak needs both, because some office workers are already hunting protein snacks and most are not.

A feed also gives **repeated exposure**: one view rarely creates desire, and ten quiet views can. That is useful for a new category. It is a poor way to explain a 40-page security product.

## A Channel Is Not a Logo

- **Official Definition:** A **channel** is a type of customer behaviour you can buy access to. A **platform** is one company’s product inside that behaviour.
- **In Simple Words:** “Search” is the behaviour. Google is a shop inside it. “Social feed” is the behaviour. Instagram is one room.
- **Real-Life Example:** Meta is not one behaviour. It is a family: Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp, and Threads. You still have to say which behaviour you are buying.

Judge the behaviour, not the fame of the app:

| Behaviour | What the person is doing | What you get | What you risk |
|-----------|--------------------------|--------------|---------------|
| **Search** | Actively looking | High intent | You only meet people who already care |
| **Social feed** | Casual discovery | Visual storytelling, broad reach, retargeting | Attention is thin. Most ads are scrolled past. |
| **Email** | Opening a box they already gave you | Direct access, useful for people who know you | A cold inbox feels like an intrusion |
| **WhatsApp** | A personal chat | Very high attention | Easy to irritate. Strangers hate surprise pitches. |
| **Calling** | A live conversation | Human qualification | Expensive per conversation |
| **LinkedIn** | Professional identity | Job, company, and business context | Costly, and not every buyer lives there |
| **Direct message or InMail** | One named person | Deep personalisation | Research time, data, and sales salary |

**Meta** fits broad reach, video, interest-based discovery, and retargeting. **Snapchat** can fit a younger visual audience, and it is a weak place to ask an 18-to-24-year-old for **₹10,000**. **LinkedIn** fits when the job title is the point.

Dating apps fail the FitFataak test. People open them for one purpose and skip ads. The age can look right while the **behaviour** is wrong.

## A-I-C-V-S: Five Tests Before You Pick a Channel

- **Official Definition:** **A-I-C-V-S** scores a channel on **Audience**, **Intent**, **Complexity**, **Value**, and **Scale** before you commit budget.
- **In Simple Words:** Five nos. If two of them fail, do not romance the logo.
- **Real-Life Example:** Cold-calling every possible buyer of a **₹500** pair of headphones fails Value and Scale. Cold-calling a short list of security buyers for a **₹30 lakh** contract can pass both.

| Letter | Question | A failure looks like |
|--------|----------|----------------------|
| **A — Audience** | Are my customers actually here? | Teen buyers on LinkedIn |
| **I — Intent** | How close are they to solving this problem? | A snack ad on a dating app |
| **C — Complexity** | Can this format explain the product? | A long technical essay on Instagram |
| **V — Value** | What is one conversion worth, after the cost of the conversation? | A human call to sell a ₹500 item |
| **S — Scale** | How many people must I reach, and does this room hold them? | A niche app when you need tens of lakhs of viewers to get 100 buyers |

![A-I-C-V-S channel test — audience, intent, complexity, value, and scale](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session06/s06-03-aicvs.png?v=20261012)

**Value** is what one conversion is worth, and **scale** is how many conversations you need versus how many a human can make in a day. A ₹500 headphone is simple, and calling still fails, because the pay and the headcount do not fit. A security product is complex, the buyers are few, and one conversation can be worth more than a month of calling cost.

**Activity: Score One Channel**

Pick either “Instagram reels for FitFataak” or “cold call for a ₹30 lakh security product.” Give each of A, I, C, V, and S a pass or a fail in one line. If Value or Scale fails, drop the channel even if the logo is famous.

## Let the Product Change the Mix

As **price** and **explanation** go up, a human usually has to enter the path. A single “Buy now” post will not close a ₹30 lakh security deal. A salesperson will not profitably dial every headphone buyer.

Use this as a starting mix, then let real behaviour override it:

| Situation | Likely mix | Why |
|-----------|------------|-----|
| Low value, simple, demand exists | Social, search, marketplace | The buyer can decide alone |
| Low value, **new category** | Social, creators, video | People must first notice the category |
| High value, demand already exists | Search, content, a sales team | They are comparing. Help them finish. |
| High value and complex | LinkedIn, **account-based marketing**, email, calls | One account is worth a tailored path |
| People who already bought | Email, WhatsApp, CRM | They have given you the right to return |
| Local, needed today | Search, Maps, “near me,” a phone call | A plumber is not a brand campaign |

FitFataak sits in the second row, because baked Indian protein namkeen is still a **new way of seeing the category**. Creators and short video have to make that picture familiar. Search and marketplaces catch the people who then go looking.

Premium running shoes above **₹15,000** sit in the third row, because people already buy the category. You earn and buy search, and you publish proof. A sales conversation can still help the expensive pair.

**Account-based marketing (ABM)** is the fourth row in practice.

- **Official Definition:** **ABM** treats each target company as its own small market and changes the message to match that company’s stage.
- **In Simple Words:** You do not spray one ad at 2,000 logos. You ask where *this* logo is in the buying story.
- **Real-Life Example:** Some accounts have never heard of you. Some already use a rival. Some are ready and only lack confidence. A cloud offer should not tell all of them to book a demo.

One enterprise account can be worth a very large yearly contract, and these buyers do not switch lightly, so a short tailored list can still be rational. The unaware account hears trust and “this product exists.” The account on a rival hears why a switch is worth the pain. The almost-ready account hears commercials and how to start.

## Audience Behaviour Is the Reality Check

Suppose the buyer is a **CFO**. Entertainment-led Instagram ads for a work product rarely hold them, because they read industry reports, search for a named problem, listen to peers, and let a team evaluate vendors. The mix leans to **search, LinkedIn, thought leadership, email, events, and direct outreach**.

Now sell **₹1,000 sneakers** to college students, and the same mix is a costume. Their attention is on Instagram, Snapchat, creators, and marketplace search. If the shoe is cheap enough, capture on a marketplace can also close the sale.

Follow attention. Then check buying power. A young Snapchat audience can be perfect for discovery and useless for a ₹10,000 professional product.

**Direct outreach** (calls, WhatsApp, LinkedIn, InMail, cold email) can say that you noticed someone runs digital marketing and is trying to automate it with AI, that you have done this for similar enterprises, and that you would like a conversation. That line costs research, data, and salary, and it pays only when the account is worth a lot or the list is short, such as 5,000 CFOs rather than 5 million snack buyers. Existing customers are the exception, because a reorder note is not a stranger’s pitch.

## Use a Portfolio, and Do Not Crown the Last Click

The useful question is not “Which one channel?” A working GTM gives three jobs to different channels: **generate** interest, **capture** intent, and **convert** the person who is ready. A typical business path is LinkedIn content, then Google Search, a landing page, a short email series, and a sales call.

A B2B sale often takes **about 90 days**, sometimes close to a year, so six or seven emails are normal. If sales cannot yet explain the product, do not turn the tap on, even in a company with 40 or 45 products. Credit is shared across LinkedIn, the page, search, and the salesperson, so the last click is not the whole win.

**Return on ad spend (ROAS)** keeps the portfolio honest.

- **Official Definition:** **ROAS** is the revenue produced by a campaign divided by what that campaign spent.
- **In Simple Words:** For every rupee of ads, how many rupees came back.
- **Real-Life Example:** Launching every FitFataak channel on Monday feels busy. If the landing page is broken, ROAS collapses and you learn nothing except that you were loud.

## Ask AI to Compare Channels, Then to Attack the Plan

AI is useful for **hypotheses**: where this audience might spend time, what they might search, what might trigger a buy, which formats fit, and what a rival seems to do. It is a poor oracle of “the best channel.”

Give it the offer and the buyer. Ask for a map across **generation, capture, and conversion**, with the job of each channel and the assumption a human must still check. Then force a comparison, because GTM is usually a trade-off.

```text
I am selling an enterprise cybersecurity product to Indian companies with 500+ employees. Primary buyers are CISOs and IT heads.
Map channels across demand generation, demand capture, and conversion.
For each channel, state the job it does and the assumption I must validate. Do not pick a winner.

Then compare Google Search, LinkedIn Ads, email outreach, and cold calling
on audience fit, intent, scalability, personalisation, cost, and buying-stage relevance.
Highlight trade-offs. Do not select a winner.
```

“Use LinkedIn because it is professional and people will engage” is not a decision, because LinkedIn is also expensive. If engagement is thin, the assumption fails and the plan should change. Test the assumption, then spend.

AI is trained to agree with you, and it will tailor a flattering answer if you only ask for a plan. The second prompt is the actual work.

```text
Act as a sceptical growth leader. Review this GTM snapshot.
Find audience-channel mismatches, missing buying stages,
over-reliance on one channel, weak assumptions,
and any place where customer behaviour contradicts the plan.
Do not rewrite until I ask.
```

Only after that:

```text
Revise the GTM snapshot using only the strongest recommendations.
Keep the segment and the positioning unchanged.
Mark every change you made and why.
```

The loop is **draft → challenge → revise**. It is not prompt → paste → slides.

## The One-Page GTM Snapshot

A snapshot is one page a busy person will reopen, not a 47-page document. It records the segment, the problem, and the positioning you already earned, then adds demand state, channels, the job of each channel, the activities, and one primary KPI. Positioning stays the previous line, Indian taste, protein, and convenience for the 4 p.m. gap.

| Block | FitFataak |
|-------|-----------|
| Segment | Urban office professionals, 25–40 |
| Problem | Unhealthy 4 p.m. snacking |
| Positioning | Indian taste + protein + convenience |
| Generate demand | Instagram and creator content that shows the 4:30 p.m. moment |
| Capture demand | Google Search and marketplaces, for people who then look |
| Convert | Retargeting and a landing page |
| Retain | Email and WhatsApp reorder journeys |
| Primary outcome | Repeatable customer acquisition, not a single viral day |

Message still has to **change shape** when the channel changes. A reel can carry “the 4 p.m. hunger hits differently,” a marketplace title has to carry protein, baked, and flavour in a few words, and a reorder WhatsApp cannot sound like a first introduction. Same positioning, different format.

## The Customer Is in a Stage, so the Message Changes

![Each stage has its own question and metric, from why care through should I stay](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session06/s06-05-stage-metrics.png?v=20261012)

Attention is not a customer, and a thousand people can watch a FitFataak ad and buy nothing. New-age commerce companies learn this when awareness is high and profit is not. You still have to **break even**.

Some people have never heard the name, some still think a protein tub is enough, and some find ₹60 expensive next to The Whole Truth or regular namkeen, while others are ready and only ask whether they will buy again. A first phone at 13 is not what matters after a child arrives. Buyers deserve that same change of message.

### Awareness — Why Should I Care?

The person does not know you, and may not know the problem is solvable, so do not open with a demo link. The message calls out the problem, for FitFataak the 4 p.m. hunger rather than a price. The desired action is to **engage**, not to buy.

Judge reach, relevant engagement, video completion (including how many watched halfway), **qualified traffic** (visitors who could actually buy), **brand search** (people typing your name), and **share of voice** (how much of the category conversation you occupy). Search Console shows whether branded queries are growing. At this stage, do not judge yourself by orders.

### Consideration — Why You, and Not the Other Snack?

They know the problem, and they are asking whether it is good, what is different, whether ₹60 is worth it, and whether they can trust it.

- Show the benefit: about **15 g protein**, baked, Indian flavours.
- Compare fairly. Against fried namkeen: oil and empty calories. Against a protein tub: this is the hour you still want something you enjoy. Against chocolate: this is a savoury snack, not a sweet.
- Add reviews and educational proof. Other customers are the authority you do not have on day one.
- **Desired action:** Explore. **Metrics:** time on the product page, **bounce rate** (they left after one page and did nothing next), return visits, content downloads, lead **quality**, add-to-cart, and enquiries. GA4 can hold these. A lead that cannot buy is not a win.

### Decision — Why Now, and Why With You?

The question wants an action, so remove risk with proof, a case study, an offer, a trial, a demo, clear pricing, an FAQ, and a sales conversation when the price needs a human. A small consumer version of the same idea is “buy three plasters for ₹159,” or a first service free. The desired action is **buy or contact**, judged by conversion rate, **CAC**, qualified opportunities, revenue, sales-cycle length, and **cart completion**.

- **Official Definition:** **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)** is the total cost of winning one new customer.
- **In Simple Words:** How much you spent, ads plus people, to get one person to pay.
- **Real-Life Example:** If only high earners can buy the flat you sell, an enquiry from someone far below that income is not a qualified opportunity. CAC that counts those enquiries as “almost customers” is a vanity number.

**Cart completion** runs from a like, to the cart, to payment, and to delivery, and **cash on delivery** fails more often than prepaid, so many brands discount prepaid. **Return to origin (RTO)** is a shipment that comes back because the customer refused it or was not there. In Indian e-commerce, RTO can erase a campaign that looked profitable at “order placed.”

### Experience and Retention

| Stage | Customer question | Your job | Desired action |
|-------|-------------------|----------|----------------|
| Awareness | Why care? | Make the problem felt | Engage |
| Consideration | Why you? | Educate and show the **point of difference** | Explore |
| Decision | Why now? | Remove risk. Show the cost of waiting. | Buy or contact |
| Experience | Did it work? | Deliver the value you promised | Use |
| Retention | Should I continue? | Reinforce the value | Repeat |

**Point of parity** keeps you in the category, and **point of difference** is why they pick you. Consideration is where the difference has to be visible, which you already separated in the previous lesson. Do not collapse them into one slogan for every stage.

The cybersecurity version of “same customer, different message”:

- **Awareness:** “Most breaches begin with a compromised user.”
- **Consideration:** “See how behaviour-based security reduces identity risk.”
- **Decision:** “Compare deployment time, coverage, and commercial impact.”

“Book a demo” sent to someone who has never felt the problem is a broken GTM. The message skipped two questions.

For a course, experience means the person actually attends, and retention means they stay. A home-services brand can expand from a plumber visit to appliance repair or a maid. Where there is only one course per field, **usage and retention** matter more than a second sale.

## A Launch Is a Sequence, Not a Monday

If LinkedIn, Google, email, creators, and sales all go live on day one, you cannot see what caused what, the customer meets you everywhere before they understand you, and you spend before you know ROAS.

![Launch is a sequence — foundation, awareness, capture, convert, then optimise](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session06/s06-04-launch-sequence.png?v=20261012) The pipes must already exist: tracking, the landing page, the CRM, and someone who can answer. Demand that arrives before those pipes is wasted, not momentum.

### Five Phases

| Phase | What must be true | FitFataak-shaped work |
|-------|-------------------|------------------------|
| **1. Foundation** | Audience, offer, tracking, page, CRM, sales or support ready | The 4 p.m. page loads, and you can see who arrived |
| **2. Awareness** | New people meet the problem | Social, short video, creators |
| **3. Capture** | People who lean in can find you | Search, retargeting, high-intent proof (reviews) |
| **4. Convert** | Ready people are helped to act | Email, WhatsApp, offers, and a human if the price needs one |
| **5. Optimise** | Money moves toward what worked | New messages, retargeting, scale the winners |

Creators are not one blob. Brands roughly sort them as **nano** (about 1,000–10,000 followers), **micro** (10,000–100,000), **macro** (100,000–1 million), **mega** (above 1 million), then **celebrities**. A new snack usually learns more from nano and micro creators than from a celebrity on week one.

### A Timeline Makes Dependencies Visible

| When | What happens |
|------|----------------|
| Week 1 | Assets, tracking, landing page |
| Week 2 | Awareness turns on |
| Week 3 | Search and retargeting |
| Week 4 | Nurture and any sales motion |
| Weeks 5–6 | Message tests and optimisation |
| Weeks 7–8 | Scale the audiences and channels that earned it |

Awareness may need **three or four weeks** before search volume is real. Starting search does not mean pausing awareness. If 20 lakh people are warm enough to nurture, you may still need on the order of **80 lakh** in awareness so that pool does not dry up.

**Creative fatigue** is what happens when the same ad is seen so often that people skip on sight, the way a life-insurance creative does after months of repetition. Optimisation includes retiring tired creatives, not only buying more reach.

**Lookalike audiences** are how you scale a winner.

- **Official Definition:** A **lookalike audience** is a group a platform builds by finding new people who resemble customers who already converted.
- **In Simple Words:** You hand over your best 100 buyers. The platform looks for their cousins.
- **Real-Life Example:** If FitFataak’s buyers are 27, urban, and already watching food reels, the lookalike hunt starts from those traits, then from whatever else those buyers have in common. You scale that. You do not scale the ad that only collected curiosity.

## A Firecracker Is Not an Engine

A launch spike looks like this: traffic jumps, then collapses, then someone asks “what next?” **Sustained acquisition** keeps feeding the next stage.

**New audiences → engaged audiences → high-intent audiences → customers → repeat customers.**

That chain is an engine. A firecracker spends everything on the first bang. Judge the launch by whether the next stage received people, and the ongoing plan by whether they stayed.

Every stage keeps its own success test:

| Stage | Ask | Do not ask |
|-------|-----|------------|
| Awareness | Reach, relevant engagement, video completion, brand interest | “Why no sales yet?” |
| Consideration | Engaged sessions, content consumed, return visits, qualified leads | “Why is CAC not final?” |
| Decision | Conversion, CAC, opportunities, revenue | “Why is reach the only slide?” |
| Retention | Repeat purchase, usage, **churn**, retention rate, customer value | “Why did week-one ROAS drop?” |

- **Official Definition:** **Churn** is the share of customers who leave in a period. **Retention rate** is the share who stay.
- **In Simple Words:** Churn is the leak. Retention is who is still in the bucket.
- **Real-Life Example:** A subscription that wins cheap signups and then loses most of them within two weeks does not have an awareness problem. It has an early-use problem.

### Vanity Metrics and Diagnostic Metrics

- **Official Definition:** A **vanity metric** can be large and still tell you nothing about the health of the system. A **diagnostic metric** tells you *where* the system is leaking.
- **In Simple Words:** “2 million impressions” is a crowd. It is not a diagnosis.
- **Real-Life Example:** High impressions and low clicks usually mean the message is not the audience’s message. The ad arrived. It did not resonate.

| What you see | What to suspect first |
|--------------|------------------------|
| High impressions, low clicks | Message or audience mismatch |
| High clicks, low conversion | Landing page, offer, or price shock |
| Many leads, few sales | Lead quality, product fit, or the sales conversation |
| Good acquisition, poor retention | The product or the promise does not survive use |

“Lose 12 kg in a month” on the ad, and “this costs ₹1 crore” on the page, will produce clicks and no customers. The leak is the promise, not the media buyer. Fix the stage that broke instead of buying more of the stage that already worked.

### Acquisition Is the Door, Not the House

After the first payment, watch six things. **Activation** and **usage** ask whether they started and kept showing up, such as attending class. **Retention**, **repeat**, **expansion**, and **churn** ask whether they stay, buy again, spend more, or go inactive, as when a plumber visit becomes a maid booking or the customer never returns.

A brand that celebrates acquisition and then says goodbye has not built a business. Retention decides how valuable the customer becomes.

**Customer lifetime value** makes that concrete.

- **Official Definition:** **Customer lifetime value (LTV or CLV)** is the total money a customer is expected to pay you over the whole relationship.
- **In Simple Words:** Not the first bill. The stack of bills if they stay.
- **Real-Life Example:** A pack or a plan at about **₹500** a month is ₹500 today, about **₹6,000** if they renew for a year, and about **₹15,000** if they stay three years. A streaming habit at $10 a month is $120 a year for the same reason. The first invoice hid the real number.

Now compare two campaigns. CAC alone will lie.

| | Campaign A | Campaign B |
|--|------------|------------|
| CAC | ₹500 | ₹700 |
| Customers acquired | 10,000 | 8,000 |
| What happens next | 70% never buy again | 60% buy repeatedly |

A looks cheaper and bigger. B can still win once repeat rate, margin, and lifetime value are on the table, because cheap acquisition becomes expensive when people never return. You cannot know that from CAC.

## Key Takeaways

- A GTM plan is **audience → proposition → channel → activity → measurement**. Starting from a favourite platform produces a shopping list. **Capture** meets existing intent. **Generate** creates attention in people who are not searching, and a higher-priced purchase usually comes back through capture before it closes.
- Score channels with **A-I-C-V-S**, then let price and complexity change the mix. Social and creators introduce a new snack category. Search, content, LinkedIn, ABM, and calls take over when the sale is expensive or hard to explain. AI should compare trade-offs and **attack** the snapshot. It should not name a winner you paste into a slide.
- The FitFataak page keeps the segment and the positioning you already set, then assigns each channel a job: generate on Instagram and creators, capture on search and marketplaces, convert with retargeting and a page, retain with email and WhatsApp.
- A launch is a **sequence** with a foundation underneath it. An ongoing plan is an **engine**: identify, position, communicate for the stage, activate on the right channel, nurture, convert, and retain. **Impressions** without a diagnosis are vanity. **LTV, churn, and repeat** tell you whether the first sale was worth it.

Later work can turn this one-page snapshot into a live calendar: creative tests, budget shifts, and a retention motion that protects the value you just acquired.

## Important Commands, Libraries & Terminologies

| Term | What it means |
|------|----------------|
| **Go-to-market (GTM)** | How the offer reaches the chosen customer, through which activities, judged by which numbers |
| **GTM chain** | Audience → proposition → channel → activity → measurement |
| **Demand capture** | Being present where people are already searching or shopping |
| **Demand generation** | Creating awareness, interest, and need among people who are not searching |
| **In-market** | Actively shopping. Not-in-market means the problem is unrecognised or unnamed |
| **Sponsored result** | A paid placement on a keyword someone already typed |
| **SEO** | Earning search visibility without paying per click. Slower than a bid |
| **Channel vs platform** | A behaviour (search, feed, inbox) versus one company’s product inside it |
| **A-I-C-V-S** | Audience, intent, complexity, value of one conversion, and whether the channel can hold the scale you need |
| **ABM** | Account-based marketing: a different message per company’s buying stage |
| **ROAS** | Revenue from the campaign divided by ad spend |
| **Multi-touch credit** | Several channels share one sale. The last click is not the whole story |
| **GTM snapshot** | One page: segment, problem, positioning, demand state, channels, roles, activities, primary KPI |
| **Draft → challenge → revise** | Ask AI for a plan, force it to find gaps, then edit. Do not paste the first draft |
| **Awareness / consideration / decision** | Why care? Why you? Why now? |
| **Point of parity / point of difference** | What keeps you in the category / what makes you the specific choice |
| **Bounce rate** | Visits that leave after a single page with no meaningful next step |
| **Qualified traffic or lead** | A visitor or enquiry that could actually buy |
| **Brand search / share of voice** | People typing your name / how much of the category conversation you occupy |
| **CAC** | Cost to acquire one customer |
| **Cart completion** | Like → cart → pay → deliver, without a failed or refused order |
| **RTO** | Return to origin: the shipment comes back undelivered |
| **Launch sequence** | Foundation → awareness → capture → convert → optimise |
| **Lookalike audience** | New people who resemble those who already converted |
| **Creative fatigue** | The same ad seen so often that people skip it on sight |
| **Activation / usage / churn** | They started / they keep using it / the share who leave |
| **LTV (CLV)** | Total revenue expected from a customer over the relationship |
| **Vanity vs diagnostic metric** | A big number that explains nothing / a number that shows where the system leaks |
