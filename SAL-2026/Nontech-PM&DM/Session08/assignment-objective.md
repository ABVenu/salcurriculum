## Q1 (MCQ, Easy)

A growth lead pastes the **same 120-word email paragraph** into a Google search headline field, a LinkedIn post, and a WhatsApp broadcast.

What mistake is this?

A. The paragraph is too long for any digital channel.
B. The team skipped **multichannel architecture**—same words, but each channel needs different structure, length, and behaviour fit.
C. AI cannot rewrite copy once an email draft exists.
D. Search ads must always use video, so text paste is invalid.

**Correct Answer:** B

**Answer Explanation:**  
**Ctrl+C marketing** ignores that LinkedIn is casual scroll, search is high intent, and WhatsApp is intimate and low tolerance. One proposition can stay; the **communication architecture** must change per channel.

**Why other options are wrong:**  
- A: Length alone is not the core error—the same long block is wrong even where length limits differ.
- C: Humans and AI can adapt copy; the failure is strategic paste, not tool limits.
- D: Search text ads are standard; video is not required.

## Q2 (MCQ, Easy)

You are briefing AI for a **WhatsApp** replenishment nudge to opted-in buyers.

Which guardrail set matches how the channel works?

A. Open with “Dear Valued Customer,” use two CTAs, and aim for 200 words.
B. Lead with a three-paragraph company history, then the offer.
C. Stay near **50 words**, **one CTA**, and **no formal salutation**.
D. Use the same hook as a LinkedIn thought-leadership post without changes.

**Correct Answer:** C

**Answer Explanation:**  
WhatsApp is high attention but low tolerance: short, contextual, actionable—or users block you. Class guardrails: ~50 words, one CTA, no “Dear Valued Customer” openings.

**Why other options are wrong:**  
- A: Formal tone and dual CTAs fight the channel norms taught in notes.
- B: Company history first violates customer-out and channel brevity.
- D: LinkedIn scroll behaviour differs from WhatsApp intimacy.

## Q3 (MCQ, Easy)

Someone is **casually scrolling** the LinkedIn feed between meetings.

What is the **first job** of the opening line?

A. List every product feature in bullet form.
B. Paste the full pricing table so finance can compare vendors.
C. Ask them to book a 60-minute demo in the first sentence.
D. **Capture attention** before they flick past the post.

**Correct Answer:** D

**Answer Explanation:**  
On LinkedIn feed, the reader is in social scroll mode. Copy must earn a pause—insight or tension—not an immediate hard close or spec sheet.

**Why other options are wrong:**  
- A: Feature dumps read like ads and lose scrollers.
- B: Pricing tables belong later in journey, not as feed hook.
- C: Hard demo asks before relevance fail the “why should I stop scrolling?” test.

## Q4 (MCQ, Easy)

You receive an AI draft that reads smoothly and uses impressive adjectives. Before you tweak wording for “professional” tone, what should you check **first** in the editing hierarchy?

A. Whether every factual claim is **true** and verifiable.
B. Whether the font matches the brand slide template.
C. Whether each paragraph has exactly three sentences for symmetry.
D. Whether the CTA button colour is on-brand.

**Correct Answer:** A

**Answer Explanation:**  
Edit order taught: **True** (fact) → Right (strategy) → Relevant → Logical → Clear → On-brand. Polishing tone before facts risks shipping invented stats or wrong product details.

**Why other options are wrong:**  
- B, D: Visual design is not the first editorial gate for copy truth.
- C: Symmetrical paragraphs are an “AI smell” pattern, not a primary QA step.

## Q5 (MCQ, Moderate)

An SDR’s LinkedIn InMail opens: *“Hi Rahul, we provide industry-leading digital transformation solutions.”*

Why will this underperform compared with a stronger InMail?

A. InMail cannot include the recipient’s first name.
B. It leads with **vendor credentials** instead of **why this person** and a concrete pain tied to their context.
C. LinkedIn blocks messages longer than 12 words.
D. IT buyers never read InMail on mobile devices.

**Correct Answer:** B

**Answer Explanation:**  
Strong InMail answers “why **me**?” with observed context (e.g., multi-location expansion) and a real pain—not generic “industry-leading” self-description.

**Why other options are wrong:**  
- A: Personalisation can include name; the flaw is shallow personalisation.
- C: No such universal 12-word block exists in the lesson.
- D: Device habit is not the lesson’s diagnosis for this weak opener.

## Q6 (MCQ, Moderate)

A nurture series sends five emails. Each email’s only message is **“Book a demo.”**

What best describes the series?

A. A full nurture arc covering problem, education, differentiation, proof, and action.
B. **Persistence**, not nurture—readers never get story progression.
C. Optimal WhatsApp strategy for cold audiences who never opted in.
D. The required structure for every B2C abandoned-cart flow.

**Correct Answer:** B

**Answer Explanation:**  
Nurture is a **story**: problem relevance → education → differentiation → proof → action. Five identical demo asks skip the middle and feel brochure-like.

**Why other options are wrong:**  
- A: That arc is what is missing, not what was built.
- C: WhatsApp needs opt-in and different guardrails; this describes email misuse.
- D: Abandoned-cart and nurture have different jobs; repeated demo CTAs alone are not “required.”

## Q7 (MSQ, Moderate)

You are writing a **goal-driven prompt** for a problem-aware outbound email to IT heads at companies with 500+ employees, promoting unified workforce security.

Which elements should appear in the prompt?

Select all that apply.

A. **Role**, **audience**, **proposition**, **journey stage**, **channel**, and **constraints** (banned phrases, no fake offers).
B. A single line: “Write an email about cybersecurity.”
C. Instruction to open with the **customer’s situation** rather than company history.
D. A **low-friction CTA** (e.g., checklist or short read), not a hard multi-year contract ask.

**Correct Answer:** A, C, D

**Answer Explanation:**  
Strong prompts carry marketing context (WHO–DON’T style fields), customer-out framing, and channel-appropriate CTAs. Vague one-liners produce generic, risky drafts.

**Why other options are wrong:**  
- B: Vagueness is the anti-pattern explicitly contrasted with the reference outbound email prompt.

## Q8 (MSQ, Moderate)

A teammate says an AI paragraph “sounds fine.” You are scanning for **AI smell**.

Which flags should you treat as real problems?

Select all that apply.

A. Opening with *“In today’s rapidly evolving digital landscape…”* and a neat three-bullet symmetry every time.
B. Repeated adjectives such as *“seamless seamless platform.”*
C. A specific, sourced product limit copied from approved documentation.
D. A confident statistic (*“99% of IT leaders agree…”*) with **no source** in the draft.

**Correct Answer:** A, B, D

**Answer Explanation:**  
AI smell includes formulaic intros, repetition, buzzword stacks, and **invented or unsupported facts**. Verified documentation-backed limits are what you want to keep.

**Why other options are wrong:**  
- C: Sourced, approved product detail is evidence—not a smell flag.

## Q10 (MSQ, Hard)

Before external publish, you run a **structured fact-check** on an AI-written landing page.

Which practices match the taught workflow?

Select all that apply.

A. Use **source hierarchy**: approved product docs and official site before trusting confident model tone.
B. Treat **“fastest,” “only,” or uptime guarantees** as claims that need verification or deletion.
C. Assume a citation URL is valid if the model formatted it like a real link—**open and check** each link.
D. Skip number checks if the tone sounds professional and the grammar is clean.

**Correct Answer:** A, B, C

**Answer Explanation:**  
Fact-check layers cover numbers, superlatives, product features, dates, and links. Hierarchy and human verification beat fluency. Tone and grammar do not replace evidence.

**Why other options are wrong:**  
- D: “Sounds professional” is exactly how invented facts ship; numbers and superlatives still need a ledger pass.
