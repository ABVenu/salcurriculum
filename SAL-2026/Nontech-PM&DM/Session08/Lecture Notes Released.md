# AI for Everyday Work: Writing Copy & Content
[PPT Used in the Live Session](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session08/AI-for-Everyday-Work-Writing-Copy-and-Content.pdf)

## Why copy still needs a human even when AI writes fast

A marketer often needs **eight formats from one brief** before the deadline: a LinkedIn post, LinkedIn InMail, search ad, email, WhatsApp, sales call opening, nurture sequence, and landing-page copy. Without AI, that feels like eight separate writing jobs. With AI, one strong strategic input can produce **multiple contextual drafts** in minutes.

The trap is **Ctrl+C marketing**: the same paragraph pasted into WhatsApp, a search ad, and a cold call.

LinkedIn is casual scrolling; Google is search with intent; email is someone opening an inbox on purpose. WhatsApp is a personal space; a call is someone deciding whether to hang up. Same product idea, different behaviour, different copy.

![Ctrl+C marketing versus one core proposition adapted for LinkedIn, email, WhatsApp, and search — same idea, different channel copy](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session08/s08-01-ctrl-c-vs-multichannel.png?v=20261027)

In previous work you already decided **who** the audience is, **why** they should choose you (positioning), **where** they will find you, and **when** to speak. Those decisions still sit underneath every line you generate here. This lesson is the writing layer: goal-driven prompts, channel structure, iterative variations, then human editing for tone, audience, facts, and nuance.

You will use two practice worlds in parallel:

- **B2B live example (Priya):** a **cloud / workforce-security** launch for IT heads at Indian companies with **500+ employees**.
- **B2C carry-forward (FitFataak):** the protein-rich snack whose positioning is **taste without giving up the healthy, lower-calorie promise** — the unique flavour of **bhujia combined with protein**.

AI’s advantage is **speed across secondary research** and **pattern matching** (what tended to work or fail in similar campaigns). It is still built by humans. You remain the editor who decides what is true, on-brand, and worth publishing.

## One strategy, many channel jobs

- **Official Definition:** **Multichannel copy** is the same strategic proposition expressed with a **different communication architecture** for each channel.
- **In Simple Words:** Keep the spine. Change the clothes.
- **Real-Life Example:** “Secure employees across locations without increasing IT complexity” can be a search headline, a LinkedIn insight, an email question, a WhatsApp checklist, an InMail observation, or a call discovery question.

### Channel behaviour (why paste-fail happens)

| Channel | What the person is doing | What copy must do |
|---------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| **LinkedIn feed** | Casual scroll; social updates | **Capture attention** before they flick past |
| **Google search** | High intent; looking for an answer | Help them **land on you** |
| **Email** | They opened the inbox on purpose | Earn the next click without shouting |
| **WhatsApp** | Personal, intimate space | Short, contextual, actionable — or they block you |
| **Call** | Deciding whether to hang up | Earn **20 more seconds** in the first few seconds |

**Pattern break** (used in social ads) is anything that interrupts the expected scroll — like a water bottle falling mid-class, or a line such as “This is not a Zomato ad.” The eye stops because the pattern broke. Attractive is not the same as random shock.

### B2C and B2B: same channels, different jobs

A common mistake is “email is B2B” or “Instagram is B2C.” Ask instead: **what role does this channel play in the journey?**

| | **B2C** (Maggi, cream, FitFataak) | **B2B** (payroll software, cloud storage, Priya’s security tool) |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | Large (lakhs to crores) | Narrow buying group (thousands of decision-makers) |
| Journey | Often short (Maggi in the next hour) | Often **6–9 months** |
| Decision | Individual / household | Many stakeholders; must **justify** spend |
| Driver | Emotion and habit (Maggi vs other noodles; Aashirvaad; Saffola) | Business case and organisational impact |
| Volume vs value | High transaction volume; personal use | Higher value per conversion |

A product can be both (festival **bulk chocolate** to companies and to individuals). The channel still plays a different job in each journey.

## How each format actually works

### Email — continued permission

People say email is outdated. It sits in a **soft spot**: less noisy than a scrolling feed, less intimate than WhatsApp or a cold call. The reader came to read mail, and you keep **permission to write again**.

**B2C:** offers, **abandoned cart**, product discovery (FitFataak is live), replenishment, loyalty, re-engagement. **B2B:** lead nurture after a form fill, white papers, event follow-up, case studies, post-demo education. Not “same newsletter every Tuesday.”

### WhatsApp — high attention, low tolerance

Copy implication: **shorter + contextual + actionable**. Never “Dear Valued Customer, we are delighted to apprise you…”

**B2C:** order updates, offers, reminders, replenishment, support. **B2B:** follow up only when the lead **gave a number** (form, RSVP); seller-assisted journeys work after opt-in.

Guardrail: **~50 words, one CTA, no formal salutation.**

### LinkedIn InMail / DM — “Why are you messaging *me*?”

Personalisation is not only name + company.

Weak: “Hi Rahul, we provide industry-leading digital transformation solutions.” (Looks at *us*.)

Better: “Hi Rahul, noticed your team is expanding across three locations. We work with IT teams trying to secure distributed users without adding separate security tools.” (Shows **context** + a real pain: extra hardware / extra complexity.)

### Calls — a conversation framework, not a theatre script

AI is useful **before** the call, not only as a writer. Build:

1. **Opening** — earn 20 more seconds after “hello.”
2. **Discovery questions** — what must I understand first?
3. **Objection routes** — what might they challenge?
4. **Proof points** — who else had this problem?
5. **Next step** — meeting, trial, intro to a senior — one ask.

Do not write a monologue the seller reads word for word.

### Nurture — a story, not five “book a demo” brochures

| Email | Job | Question it answers |
|-------|-----|---------------------|
| 1 | Problem relevance | Why should I care? |
| 2 | Education | How big is this problem? |
| 3 | Differentiation | Why this approach? |
| 4 | Proof | Has it worked elsewhere? |
| 5 | Action | What should I do next? |

Five emails that only say **book a demo** are persistence, not nurture. In class, strong InMails and nurtures arrived as a story: problem → other companies → how they solved it → proof → only then the meeting ask.

### Ads — almost no patience

You have a few seconds. The instruction to AI must include **platform** (Meta, Google, LinkedIn), **format** (single image, carousel, video), **length**, **audience** (CXO vs 18–24), **objective** (visit, lead, call), **stage** (unaware, problem-aware, abandoned cart), and **CTA** (read more, call now, open the app).

Store those fields in a ChatGPT **project** or Claude **skill** so every ad brief asks them first.

## Channel structure is architecture, not word count

Do not only tell AI “make it shorter.” Tell it to change the **structure**.

| Channel | Typical structure |
|---------|-------------------|
| Email | Subject → Hook → Value → Proof → CTA |
| WhatsApp | Context → Value → Action |
| LinkedIn post | Hook → Insight → Evidence → Takeaway |
| InMail | Relevance → Problem → Value → Next step |
| Search ad | Intent → Benefit → Action |
| Social ad | Pattern break → Problem → Promise → CTA |
| Call | Permission → Discovery → Value → Next step |
| Nurture | Educate → Build confidence → Progress |

![One core proposition branching into channel-specific structures — email, WhatsApp, LinkedIn, InMail, search, and call each with its own flow](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session08/s08-02-channel-architecture.png?v=20261027)

### Same product, seven expressions

**Core proposition:** Secure employees across locations without increasing IT complexity.

- **Search:** Secure hybrid workforces without tool sprawl  
- **LinkedIn:** Your workforce went hybrid. Your security stack probably multiplied with it.  
- **Email:** Is hybrid work making security harder to manage?  
- **WhatsApp:** Managing security across office + remote teams? Here’s a quick checklist for reducing tool sprawl.  
- **InMail:** Noticed your organisation has a distributed workforce. Curious how you’re handling security across users and locations.  
- **Call:** “How are you currently securing employees working outside the corporate network?”

FitFataak spine: **evening hunger solved without compromising taste or piling on empty calories.** Search can be intent; WhatsApp a replenishment nudge; LinkedIn mainly if you talk to retail buyers.

**Activity:** Write seven openings from one positioning sentence. Keep the idea; change opening, length, and CTA.

A **product listing** is another format, not a seventh slogan. Marketplace copy is scanned: title (search keywords), 3–5 benefit bullets, one proof line, one CTA. Example FitFataak title spine: “Protein bhujia — evening snack, not empty calories.” Do not dump the 120-word email onto the listing page.

```text
Write a marketplace product listing for FitFataak protein bhujia.
Audience: urban professionals who want an evening snack without empty calories.
Include: title under 80 characters, 4 benefit bullets, one proof line (flag if unverified), one CTA.
Do not use “best-in-class”, “unlock”, or health claims we cannot source.
```

## Prompt with marketing context, not “write an email”

Weak: “Write an email about cybersecurity.”

Give AI **role, audience, context, proposition, journey stage, channel, goal, constraints**. Constraints include what it must **not** do: no offer you do not have, no competitor names, no banned words.

You do not teach the model English; you give it **marketing context**. Voice dictation of a long prompt is valid. Length is not the enemy; vagueness is.

### Reference prompt: outbound email (as used in class)

```text
You are a B2B copywriter creating an outbound email for IT heads at Indian companies with 500+ employees.
They are managing hybrid workforces and may be struggling with multiple disconnected security tools.
Our proposition is unified workforce security with simpler policy management.
The reader is problem-aware but does not know our brand.
Write an introductory email under 120 words. Use a professional but conversational tone.
Begin with the customer's situation rather than our company. Include one low-friction CTA.
Avoid exaggerated claims and phrases such as “cutting-edge”, “transform”, and “best-in-class”. Avoid em dashes.
```

**Low-friction CTA** = an easy next step (a checklist, a 10-minute read), not “sign a three-year contract.” **Customer-out** = start with their world, not your company history.

### Multi-format master prompt

Once strategy is clear, one prompt can cover the channel set:

```text
Using the customer and positioning information below, create: one email, one WhatsApp message, one LinkedIn post, one LinkedIn InMail, one search ad concept, one social ad, and one 30-second call opening.
Keep the core proposition consistent but adapt structure, length, tone and CTA to how customers behave on each channel.
After each draft, explain in one sentence what you changed because of the channel.
[Paste customer + positioning]
```

That last line forces the model to **show the adaptation logic**, not just shrink the word count.

### Channel guardrails (add these as “must not”)

- **WhatsApp:** max 50 words; one CTA; no formal salutation.  
- **LinkedIn:** lead with insight, not product; no immediate hard sell.  
- **Email:** one primary argument; one CTA; no long company intro.  
- **Calling:** not a monologue; include two discovery questions.  
- **Ads:** **one idea per variant** — not “tasty + healthy + protein + low calorie” stuffed into one FitFataak creative.

### Prompt cheat sheet (WHO → DON’T)

Before you ask AI to write, fill: **WHO** (audience), **WHY** (problem), **WHAT** (proposition), **WHERE** (channel), **WHEN** (stage), **DO** (action), **DON’T** (constraints). Then **Generate → Critique → Refine**.

## Iteration beats the perfect first prompt

Myth: “If my prompt is perfect, the first output is perfect.” You do not brief human writers that way. You **brief → review → feedback → improve**. Same loop with AI.

### Iteration 1 — raw material

```text
Generate five LinkedIn hooks around security complexity created by hybrid work.
```

Do **not** reply “make them better.” Diagnose: too generic, too dramatic, too technical, too product-heavy, wrong audience, weak insight?

### Iteration 2 — specific direction

Not “make it punchier.”

```text
Options 2 and 4 are closest. Rewrite them for an IT leader rather than a general business audience.
Remove fear-based language. Make the tension about operational complexity rather than cyberattack risk. Keep each under 14 words.
```

### Iteration 3 — GENERATE → CRITIQUE → REWRITE

Make the model the sceptical editor too:

```text
Act as a sceptical IT head receiving these messages. For each option tell me: what sounds generic, what I would distrust, what feels irrelevant, and what would make me continue reading. Then rewrite the two strongest options using that critique.
```

The cycle shortens as the chat learns your taste. It never goes to zero.

### You still choose

AI can produce 15 options, score them, and rewrite them. The marketer still picks:

- **Which insight matters** (in a snack launch, “healthy now” may beat “legacy tasty brand” even if the model prefers heritage).  
- **What is believable** (no “Shaktiman powers after one packet”).  
- **What matches the brand** (Indigo does not suddenly sell spaceships under the same name).  
- **What deserves a 10% test** before you scale.

Volume of output is not quality of judgment.

### Everyday AI content workflow

**Customer → Positioning → Channel → Objective → Generate variants → Critique → Refine → Human review → Publish / test.**

The expensive mistake is **Generate → Publish**.

![Generate, critique, refine, and human review loop — with a warning against skipping straight from generate to publish](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session08/s08-04-generate-critique-refine.png?v=20261027)

## Editing starts with tone, not “make it professional”

AI can draft at speed. Your advantage is **editorial judgment**: what stays, what changes, what gets cut.

This sentence looks finished and says almost nothing:

> In today's rapidly evolving digital landscape, businesses must embrace innovative, cutting-edge solutions to unlock unprecedented growth and stay ahead of the curve.

- **Official Definition:** **Tone** is how the brand sounds while saying the same action.
- **In Simple Words:** Four ways to ask for verification still ask for verification.
- **Real-Life Example:** Formal — “We recommend completing verification before proceeding.” Friendly — “Just verify your details and you’re ready to go.” Urgent — “Verify now to avoid interruption.” Reassuring — “It only takes a moment to verify your details.”

Telecom recharge reminders do the same: friendly at seven days, service-continuity near expiry, sharper after incoming/outgoing stop. Same CTA (recharge), different emotional temperature.

Airtel often sounds serious-professional; Vi more youthful; Jio historically tied offers to a **growth-of-India** story. Cement brands (for example Ultratech) often sound like nation-building. Pick a lane.

“Make it professional” is as vague as Barney Stinson’s “suit up.” A café can be professional in casual clothes. Give **dimensions**:

| Dimension | Example brand setting |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Formal ↔ Conversational | 70% conversational |
| Serious ↔ Playful | Mostly serious |
| Technical ↔ Simple | Simple |
| Bold ↔ Cautious | Confident, not “we are the only surf in India” |
| Expert ↔ Friendly | Expert-friend |
| Detailed ↔ Concise | Concise |

### Writing guidelines AI can apply

Instead of “sound like our brand”:

- Short sentences.  
- **Lead with the customer, not the company.**  
- Prefer **active voice** (“Main chalunga” vs “Mujhe chalaya jayega”).  
- Explain internal jargon (a “four-channel DMS dashcam with ADAS”; fridge “AI cooling” and in-built stabilizer that needed a technician to decode).  
- Avoid inflated claims; humour sparingly; never patronising; one primary idea per paragraph.  
- **Evidence over adjectives** — not title-card praise without proof.

### What the brand is *not*

Negative instructions work. Example: we **are** clear, knowledgeable, direct, useful, human. We **are not** overexcited, preachy, cute, corporate, hyperbolic.

Don’t say: “Revolutionise your digital journey.”  
Do say: “Manage all your locations from one platform.”

### Reader persona changes the same solution

Cloud migration (or Priya’s security stack):

- **CTO:** architecture, migration risk, integration, scalability.  
- **CFO:** cost, predictability, business risk, ROI.  
- **CEO:** growth, resilience, organisational impact.  
- **Employee:** what changes, what gets easier, what they must do.

Match **complexity to reader knowledge**, not to product complexity.

- **Executive version:** ~30 seconds.  
- **Practitioner version:** operational detail (if you sell an ads product to media buyers: targeting, audiences, B2B vs B2C fit).  
- **Beginner version:** simple explanation.  
- **Specialist version:** precise terms.  
- **Sales version:** outcome, proof, next step.

### Length is a strategic instruction

Do not say “shorten this.” Say:

```text
Reduce this from 180 words to 90 words while preserving the problem, differentiator, evidence, and CTA.
Remove repetition before removing substantive information. If two of those four must share a sentence, combine differentiator + evidence — never drop the problem or the CTA.
```

## Editing hierarchy and human-in-the-loop

Check in this order (do not start with “does it sound nice?”): **True** (fact) → **Right** (strategy) → **Relevant** (will this CTO / CFO / practitioner care?) → **Logical** (structure) → **Clear** (language) → **On-brand** (tone).

The marketer is not grammar police. Own **judgment** (is HD vs SD still worth saying when the era is smart boxes?), **accountability** (AI will not be fired; you will), and **audience truth**. Stay in the loop on every external send.

## Recognising “AI smell”

Fluent is not the same as useful. Flags from class:

**Structure:** formulaic intro; excessive symmetry (every para the same length); a heading for everything; generic transitions.

**Language:** inflated claims (“99% of people trusted…”); artificial enthusiasm (“we are thrilled to announce”); the word **trust** used as perfume; unsupported facts; over-punctuation; repeated sentence patterns.

**Repetition:** “Our seamless platform delivers seamless connectivity…” — the model latches onto one adjective. Fix: remove repeated nouns, adjectives, openings, and **conceptual** repetition, not only synonyms.

**Buzzwords to treat as suspects:** revolutionary, best-in-class, thrilled, unlock, next-generation, cutting-edge, transform, unprecedented, stay ahead of the curve.

**Formulaic skeleton to break:** “In today’s rapidly evolving world” → “Here’s why it matters” → “The good news?” → “Enter XYZ” → three neat bullets → “The future starts now.” One article is fine. Twenty articles and the brand is one enthusiastic robot.

**Stylistic tells:** excessive **em dashes** (the long dash that is not the hyphen key on a laptop); “it’s not X, it’s Y”; always-three-item lists; repeated contrast; artificially neat conclusions; over-bold. An em dash is not proof of AI. **Repetitive patterning** is.

**Invented facts (most dangerous):** fake statistics, outdated capabilities, fictional customers, fake studies, fake quotes, broken URLs. Courts have already called out lawyers who filed model-invented case law. Marketing can invent “India’s fastest” the same way — fluent, confident, and unusable until a human opens the source.

### Reusable “Remove the AI smell” prompt

```text
Edit the draft below so it reads like an experienced human marketer wrote it. Preserve the meaning and all verified factual claims.
Remove or rewrite: generic introductions and conclusions; repetitive sentence structures; repeated keywords or adjectives; unnecessary em dashes; overused “not X, but Y” constructions; buzzwords and corporate jargon; inflated or unsupported claims; artificial enthusiasm; filler transitions; predictable three-part lists that add no value.
Use natural sentence-length variation. Prefer specific nouns, verbs and examples over adjectives. Keep the tone [INSERT TONE] for [INSERT AUDIENCE].
Do not invent examples, statistics, quotations, links or product capabilities. Flag anything requiring factual verification instead of guessing.
After editing, list the five most important changes you made and why.
[Paste draft]
```

The goal is **better writing**, not beating an AI-detector.

### Watch one sentence get honest

Before: “In today’s fast-paced and ever-evolving business landscape, organisations need cutting-edge solutions that seamlessly empower their workforce while unlocking unprecedented productivity.”

Ask: **what does this actually mean?** Useful thought: companies need employees to work securely from different locations.

After: “Your employees work from everywhere. Their security should too.” Follow-up: apply consistent policies across office and remote users without a separate tool for every location.

Moves: generic → specific; abstract → concrete; company language → customer situation; adjectives → meaning.

## Structured edit, fact-check, then human nuance

Five passes beat “please improve this”:

1. **Meaning** — what is it actually saying?  
2. **Audience** — why would they care?  
3. **Evidence** — which claims need proof?  
4. **Language** — what can be clearer or shorter?  
5. **Voice** — does it sound like us?

### Fact-check in layers

Inspect **numbers** (stats, %, prices), **claims** (fastest, largest, leading, only — “fastest internet” needed a defined test), **dates**, **people** (names, titles, quotes), **product** (features, availability — do not announce iPhone 20), **external evidence**, **links** (ask the model for the source URL, then **open it**; 30 seconds per link).

### Product facts: source hierarchy

If AI writes “Our platform guarantees 99.999% uptime,” ask whether the **demo / docs** said that. If not, **verify or delete**.

1. Approved product documentation  
2. Official website  
3. Approved sales collateral  
4. Reliable third-party evidence (not “the model sounded confident”)

### Add real examples after the draft

Generic: “Hybrid work creates security challenges.”  
Human nuance: “Your employee may be in Gurgaon on Monday, Goa on Wednesday and an airport lounge on Friday. Your security policy still needs to follow the user.”

AI plus public internet = average. Distinctive copy adds **last-20-customer complaints, Monday sales objections, product limits, why the largest customer bought, and what every competitor already says**.

**Activity:** From one AI email, ledger every number, superlative, and feature as Verified / Unverifiable / Delete, then add one Gurgaon–Goa-style picture.

## Prompts, skills, and connectors

Once you can brief well, stop retyping the same 25 brand rules.

- **Official Definition:** A **Claude Skill** is a reusable set of instructions and files that extends Claude with a specialised workflow (an SOP for AI). A **connector** lets the model reach apps and data (Drive, Slack, ads, CRM) according to permissions.
- **In Simple Words:** Prompt = this task now. Skill = how we always do this kind of task. Connector = where the live information lives.
- **Real-Life Example:** “Write a Diwali post” is a prompt. “Every asset goes through an 18-point brand review” is a skill. “Pull last month’s Google Ads and run the ads-audit skill” is connector + skill, almost **zero extra prompt**.

In Claude, invoke with **`/skill-name`** (and skill-builder helpers). Upload open packs only if you trust them.

A skill can store tone rules, banned phrases, product names, editorial checklist, fact-check expectations, and examples. An **Airtel Business Copy Reviewer** can identify persona, preserve approved claims, check tone and CTA, strip jargon, flag unsupported stats, expand acronyms, return a cleaner draft, and explain material changes.

Other skill-worthy loops: **Google Ads audit** (structure, headlines, quality score, search terms, phrase vs exact), **LinkedIn ads**, competitor-site review, **PBT** write-ups (problem, action, impact, the biggest number and why).

| Use a **prompt** when… | Use a **skill** when… |
|------------------------|------------------------|
| One-off; context changes a lot | Repeated workflow; rules stay stable |
| Experimenting; personal task | Standardising; team / company work |
| Small instruction set; need flexibility | Detailed operating rules; need consistency |

**Trade-off:** prompts are fast and easy to tweak, but people forget instructions and output varies. Skills are shareable and consistent, but a **bad skill scales bad behaviour** — maintain it when product or brand rules change. Master prompting **before** you jump to skills.

### Skills + connectors as an operating workflow

Basic: Prompt → Draft.

Stronger: connector pulls **approved Product X** → connector pulls research → skill applies brand rules → AI drafts **three email routes** → skill runs editorial/fact-check → **human approves**.

LinkedIn campaign example: retrieve approved product info and past learnings → apply audience assumptions → generate creative territories → brand-writing skill → validate claims → human nuance → publish/test. AI sits **inside** the workflow; it does not replace it.

### The AI work stack

**Prompt** (what do I want right now?) → **Skill** (how should this recurring task run?) → **Connector** (which source of truth?) → **Human** (is this actually right?).

## Key Takeaways

- One positioning spine can feed email, WhatsApp, LinkedIn, search, InMail, ads, and calls only if you change **structure, length, opening, proof, CTA, and tone** for how people behave on that channel — not if you paste the same paragraph.
- Strong prompts carry **WHO–DON’T context** and channel guardrails; then **generate → diagnose → directed rewrite → sceptical critique**. The marketer still chooses insight, believability, brand fit, and what to test.
- Edit in order: **true, strategically right, relevant, logical, clear, on-brand**. Replace “make it professional” with tone dimensions, “we are / we are not,” and reader-specific complexity.
- **AI smell** is formula, buzzwords, repetition, and invented facts. Run the remove-smell prompt, a five-pass structured edit, and a source-hierarchy fact-check; then add proprietary human nuance.
- For repeating work, encode the SOP as a **skill** and ground it with **connectors**; keep a human on publish. In upcoming work, the same draft–verify–refine habit applies whenever copy meets live product data and brand systems.
