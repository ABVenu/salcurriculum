# Marketing Your Product - Part 1: Positioning & Messaging

## What You Will Learn in This Lesson

In the previous lesson you used an AI chat tool as an **analysis partner**. You turned a table into a plain-language summary, asked targeted questions, and verified every number before shipping a decision-ready report.

A verified insight is still only half the job. Once you know *what is happening*, you still have to decide **who you sell to** and **why those people should choose you**. This lesson is about that choice: pick a segment, name the pain, write a value proposition, lock a one-line positioning statement, then turn that strategy into words.

What you will practise, in order:

- Divide a market with **STP** (segmentation, targeting, positioning) and reject the idea that “everyone” is a customer.
- Name a **target audience**, a **buyer persona**, and the **primary pain point** you will own.
- Write a **differentiated value proposition** and a one-line **positioning statement**.
- Use AI to analyse profiles, stress-test positioning, and draft **taglines**, **landing-page headlines**, and short **value lines**.
- Evaluate copy for **tone**, **clarity**, and **resonance**, then refine it with human judgment.

The running example is **FitFataak**: a baked, high-protein, low-oil Indian namkeen-style snack, sold online at about **₹60**. Features stay the same; the reason people buy does not. Use the same workflow on **your own** product — FitFataak is the teaching example, not the only allowed brand.

## Two Questions Every Marketer Must Answer

Marketing does not start with a logo or a clever line. It starts with two questions.

- **Who should I sell to?** Not “who could theoretically eat this,” but which people are worth pursuing.
- **Why should they choose me?** If they already have samosas, chips, biscuits, and protein bars, what makes *this* the better next buy?

If you skip either question, you write generic ads. “Delicious snacks for everyone — healthy, tasty, and affordable” sounds friendly. It is not designed for anyone, so it is easy to ignore.

### Consumer versus Customer

- **Official Definition:** A **consumer** uses the product. A **customer** pays for it. They can be the same person, or two different people.
- **In Simple Words:** The person who eats the chocolate is not always the person who bought it.
- **Real-Life Example:** A child eats the chocolate (consumer). A parent pays at the counter (customer). A sixteen-year-old may love FitFataak’s taste, but the parent may still decide the purchase.

Rohan, who owns FitFataak, cannot treat “all Indians who love namkeen” as his audience. Indians do love namkeen. That is a market fact, not a targeting decision.

The same pack can be eaten for four different reasons:

| Who | What they want from the same snack |
|-----|-------------------------------------|
| 16-year-old student | Tasty and cheap — they may be the consumer, not always the customer |
| 28-year-old gym enthusiast | Protein, oil, and calories on the label |
| 40-year-old office manager | Convenience plus health during a long meeting day |
| Person binge-watching at 2 a.m. | Crunch and convenience, with almost no planning |

![Same FitFataak pack, four reasons — a student wants cheap taste, a gym-goer reads protein and oil, an office manager wants convenient health, and a late-night viewer wants crunch](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session05/s05-01-same-snack-four-reasons.png?v=20261005)

They eat the same snack. They do not eat it for the same reason. Rohan must choose **whom to appeal to** and **whom to ignore**. A friend to all is a friend to none.

**Activity: Choose Whom to Ignore**

Write one sentence: “FitFataak will not primarily sell to _____ because _____.” Use profit, market size, willingness to pay, or brand fit — not taste.

## STP: Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning

Once you accept that you cannot serve everyone, you need a method to choose. That method is **STP**.

- **Official Definition:** **STP** is a marketing framework: **segmentation** divides a market into meaningful groups, **targeting** chooses which group to serve, and **positioning** decides the idea that group should attach to your brand.
- **In Simple Words:** First cut the market into different kinds of people. Then pick your people. Then own one clear idea in their mind.
- **Real-Life Example:** A generic snack ad says “healthy, tasty, affordable.” An STP ad says “the 4 p.m. snack with 15 grams of protein that does not leave you guilty before dinner.” That second line is built for the office manager, not for “everyone.”

![STP in three moves — segmentation divides the market, targeting chooses one group, and positioning owns one idea](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session05/s05-02-stp-framework.png?v=20261005)

Without STP, the ad is a slogan. With STP, the ad points at a **pain** and offers a **specific fix**.

## Four Ways to Cut a Market

A segment is useful only if the groups are **meaningfully different**. Age alone is rarely enough. A Gen Z student in Delhi or Mumbai does not think like a Gen Z student in Odisha or Chhattisgarh if one group is still solving survival and the other is talking about lifestyle. Same age band. Different geography. Different problems.

![Four lenses for cutting a market — demographic who they are, geographic where they live, psychographic how they think, and behavioural what they do](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session05/s05-03-four-segmentation-lenses.png?v=20261005)

### Demographic Segmentation

- **Official Definition:** **Demographic segmentation** groups people by measurable facts such as age, income, gender, occupation, and family stage.
- **In Simple Words:** Who they are on paper.
- **Real-Life Example:** Double Income No Kids (DINK) households often have more disposable income than Double Income Double Kids — unless the second household earns much more. The label is a start, not the whole truth.

### Geographic Segmentation

- **Official Definition:** **Geographic segmentation** groups people by where they live: country, region, city tier, urban or rural.
- **In Simple Words:** Place changes need, even when age looks the same.
- **Real-Life Example:** Builders in a plain use a lot of cement. Builders in a hill region use more wood. Buying habits follow the land, not a national average.

### Psychographic Segmentation

- **Official Definition:** **Psychographic segmentation** groups people by lifestyle, values, aspirations, and interests — how they think, not only who they are.
- **In Simple Words:** What they want their life (or home) to say about them.
- **Real-Life Example:** Airtel Extreme cost about **₹2,000** when a regular DTH plan was **₹300–400**. A Delhi customer earning ₹17,000–18,000 bought it so guests would ask about the box. A wealthy doctor in Wazirabad already had an expensive TV and still bought Extreme because the TV “deserved” the costliest box.

Same product, two aspirations, two ads: one about social reaction (“Extreme lagaaya, toh log poochenge”), and one about “your TV deserves the best set-top box.”

### Behavioural Segmentation

- **Official Definition:** **Behavioural segmentation** groups people by what they do: usage, frequency, occasion, benefits sought, and loyalty.
- **In Simple Words:** Watch the habit, not only the age.
- **Real-Life Example:** People who snack between **4 p.m. and 6 p.m.** are an occasion FitFataak can buy media against. Uber and Ola push ride offers around 9–10 a.m. and 5–6 p.m., and on good-weather weekends, because that is when behaviour shows up.

Zomato users look like one audience until you watch behaviour:

| Behaviour | What they are doing |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Convenience seeker | Hungry now — just get food |
| Deal hunter | ₹250 biryani, coupon, then checkout |
| Food explorer | Today Korean, tomorrow Chinese |
| Family order | A large, shared meal |
| Late-night user | Binge-watch, then order |

Demographic data tells you **who they are**. Behavioural data tells you **why they buy**. Both matter. Behaviour usually writes better copy.

### Same Demographics, Different People

Aman and Karan can share the same card: **30 years old, Delhi, ₹15 lakh salary, male**.

- Aman works out six days a week and reads protein and oil labels. He sits with the gym enthusiast.
- Karan’s main exercise is finding the TV remote. He sits with the 2 a.m. Netflix snacker.

If you target “30-year-old Delhi men earning ₹15 lakh,” you have not chosen a customer. You have chosen a census row.

**Activity: Cut One Product Four Ways**

Pick FitFataak or your own product. Write one demographic cut, one geographic cut, one psychographic cut, and one behavioural cut. Circle the cut that would change your message the most.

## What Makes a Segment Worth Keeping

Not every slice of the market is a segment you should keep.

- **Official Definition:** A **good segment** is **measurable**, **substantial**, **reachable**, **differentiable**, and **actionable**.
- **In Simple Words:** You can count it, it is big enough, you can find the people, it is different from the next group, and you can do something distinct for them.
- **Real-Life Example:** “People in Karol Bagh who walk to Connaught Place and eat mutton at 4 p.m. every day” is vivid and almost certainly too small. “All males in the universe” is measurable and useless.

| Test | Question to ask | Weak answer |
|------|-----------------|-------------|
| Measurable | Can I estimate how many people this is? | A feeling, no count |
| Substantial | Is it big enough to build a business? | A handful of very specific people |
| Reachable | Can I find them on a channel I can afford? | A group I cannot legally or practically reach |
| Differentiable | Is this group different from the next one? | Two groups that need the same message |
| Actionable | Can I do something different for them? | A slice I cannot treat differently |

The goal of segmentation is **insight**, not surgical accuracy. “People aged 24 years 7 months to 26 years 2 months living exactly 3.7 kilometres away” is precise and empty.

Once a segment is real, messaging follows the need:

| Segment | Primary need | Message implication |
|---------|--------------|---------------------|
| College students | Cheap and tasty | Price and fun |
| Gym users | Protein, less oil | Intake and training fuel |
| Office professionals | Convenient and healthier | 4 p.m. without guilt |
| Parents | “Is this okay for my child?” | Health and trust |

Maggi is a clean Indian example. After the relaunch, ads aimed at **mothers** leaned on **healthy**. Ads aimed at **young audiences** leaned on **tasty** and “OG.” Same brand. Different segment. Different line.

## How You Choose a Target

Segmentation creates options. Targeting is the decision.

Score each candidate on **market size**, **strength of the problem**, **willingness to pay**, **reachability**, **how crowded the space is**, and **brand fit**. A huge group with a weak problem is a trap. So is a real problem nobody will pay to fix.

A beer-bottle opener that pours straight into a glass is a product in search of a problem. A snoring gadget may be a real irritation and still be too small a category.

If the only lever left in a crowded space is **price** — think of how many home-service apps entered after the category filled — the advantage rarely lasts. Free data did not stay free. Low launch prices did not stay low.

A simple **1–5 targeting scorecard** for FitFataak:

| Filter | Students | Gym users | Office professionals |
|--------|----------|-----------|----------------------|
| Market size | High — many students | Medium | High |
| Problem strength | Low — they want cheap and tasty, not protein | High | High |
| Willingness to pay ₹60 | Low | Higher | Higher |
| Reachability | High — they are on social | High | High |
| Crowding | High — many cheap snacks | Medium | Medium |
| Brand fit for a healthy snack | Low | High | High |

**The Whole Truth** versus **Cadbury** is the same idea in chocolate. Many students still reach for Cadbury; a health-conscious thirty-year-old who prefers dates and no added sugar may choose The Whole Truth instead. Preference and brand fit changed; the product did not have to change first.

A useful **target audience** is still a group, not a novel: *urban working professionals, 25–40, health-conscious, who snack during office hours.*

That is not yet a **buyer persona**.

- **Official Definition:** A **buyer persona** is a named, specific picture of the customer — situation, goal, pain, objection, and trigger — used to write messages you can actually ship.
- **In Simple Words:** The target audience is the neighbourhood. The persona is one person you can write to.
- **Real-Life Example:** **Hungry Harsh** is 31, works in Gurugram, gets hungry at **4:30 p.m.**, has joined a gym three times and is currently in “food time,” and wants healthy food without giving up taste.

| Field | Target audience | Hungry Harsh |
|-------|-----------------|--------------|
| Who | Urban working professionals, 25–40, health-conscious office snackers | 31, Gurugram, serial gym-joiner, 4:30 p.m. hunger |
| Goal | Eat healthier without giving up enjoyment | Same — no boiled broccoli every evening |
| Pain | Health or taste, not both | The 4 p.m. desk snack is junk, or it is boring |
| Objection | ₹60 feels expensive | “I can get a cheaper namkeen” |
| Trigger | Hunger around 4 p.m. | Hunger at 4:30 p.m., between lunch and dinner |

![A target audience is a crowd of urban professionals; a buyer persona is Hungry Harsh at 4:30 p.m. in Gurugram, wanting health and taste](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session05/s05-04-audience-vs-persona.png?v=20261005)

Age, gender, and salary tell you **who**. Goal, pain, objection, and trigger tell you **what to say** and **when to show up**.

Tata Nano is what happens when the product idea and the **positioning** fight each other. A cheaper car could have been “upgrade from two-wheeler to four-wheeler,” but calling it loudly cheap stripped the **prestige** a first family car carries in India. The segment existed; the idea attached to the car did not help the buyer feel proud.

**Activity: Score Two Segments, Then Write One Persona**

Score students versus office professionals on the six filters above (1–5). Then write a six-line persona for your winner: name, city, trigger time, goal, pain, objection.

## Positioning: The Idea You Want Them to Own

After targeting, the next question is simple: **why should they choose this?**

Customers never judge FitFataak alone. They compare it with a **samosa** (tastier), **chips** (tastier), a **biscuit** (lighter, a little less “junk”), a **protein bar** (more protein, heavier, more expensive), and whatever else is on the desk. Those are different categories. They still sit in one mental shortlist.

### Point of Parity versus Point of Difference

- **Official Definition:** A **point of parity (POP)** is a must-have that keeps you in the category. A **point of difference (POD)** is the specific thing that makes a customer choose you.
- **In Simple Words:** Parity stops you from getting eliminated. Difference wins the choice.
- **Real-Life Example:** A food-delivery app must offer restaurant choice, payment, and tracking. Tracking will not win the order. Missing tracking can lose it. **Bistro**-style delivery (a set menu, food in about 10–15 minutes instead of 40–60) is a point of difference: speed you can feel.

Ask three questions of any claimed difference: **Does the customer care?** **Can rivals copy it tomorrow?** **Can we actually deliver it, not only promise it?** “Good quality and great service” fails all three. **Airtel Priority 5G** (better speed in a crowd for some users) and **Airtel Black** (Wi-Fi, DTH, and postpaid on one bill) are specific enough to test.

Generic “flights across India” is descriptive. Anyone can say it. **IndiGo** can own **reliable, low-cost flying with a simple, no-frills experience**, and it can shout **on-time departure** because the network can back the claim.

Tide owns clean clothes. Rapido owns more bikes nearby. Zomato owns restaurant discovery. Jio owns a cost advantage. Each brand is trying to own **one idea**, not ten.

### Features, Benefits, and Value

Most teams stop at features. Customers buy value.

- **Official Definition:** A **feature** is a product fact. A **benefit** is what that fact lets the user do. **Value** is why their life is better.
- **In Simple Words:** Specs are for the spec sheet. Value is for the customer.
- **Real-Life Example:** “2 horsepower” is a feature. “Walk at home for an hour” is a benefit. “Lose weight without joining a gym” is value.

| Layer | Walking treadmill | What the customer hears |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------------|
| Feature | 2 horsepower; variable speed | Technical data |
| Benefit | Walk at home for an hour; charge in about 30 minutes | What it lets me do |
| Value | Lose weight at home without joining a gym | Why my life is better |

Run the **So What? test** until a feature becomes an outcome. “We use AI.” So what? “It analyses customer behaviour.” So what? “You get personalised recommendations.” So what? “You spend less time searching.” The last line is the line.

### The Value Proposition Canvas

- **Official Definition:** A **value proposition canvas** maps a customer’s job-to-be-done, pains, and gains to what the product actually offers.
- **In Simple Words:** Draw a line from their afternoon problem to your pack — not a poster that could fit any brand.
- **Real-Life Example:** Harsh’s job is stopping 4 p.m. hunger. His pain is “healthy is boring or expensive.” FitFataak’s gain is tasty, filling, protein-rich, and convenient.

| Canvas piece | Hungry Harsh / office professional |
|--------------|-------------------------------------|
| Job | Stop evening hunger between lunch and dinner |
| Pains | Healthy food feels expensive; fruit is not satisfying; “healthy” snacks are boring |
| Gains | Tasty, filling, protein-rich, convenient, reasonably priced |

Then write the offer in this shape:

> For **[target audience]** who **[problem]**, **[product]** provides **[benefit]** because **[reason to believe]**.

Worked FitFataak value line:

> For working professionals who get hungry between lunch and dinner but want to eat healthier, FitFataak offers tasty, protein-rich Indian snacks that are an alternative to typical office junk food.

A one-line **positioning statement** uses a slightly tighter frame so the product also names its **category**:

> For **[target market]** who **[need]**, **[product]** is a **[category]** that **[key benefit]**.

Worked FitFataak positioning:

> For urban working professionals who get hungry at 4 p.m. and refuse to choose between health and taste, FitFataak is a baked Indian protein snack that fills the gap between lunch and dinner without the guilt of office junk food.

**Positioning is internal strategy.** A **tagline** is the creative public line. “People with expensive TVs who buy the costliest box” is positioning. “Your TV deserves the best set-top box” is the tagline. Do not confuse the two.

**Activity: Write Both Lines**

Using your persona, write (1) the “For [audience] who [problem]…” value line and (2) the “For [market] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [benefit]” positioning line. Read both aloud. If a stranger cannot tell what you sell, rewrite.

## Using AI to Understand Customers — Without Letting It Invent Them

AI has read more public text than you can. That makes it a strong **assistant** for hypothesising segments, objections, and triggers. It is not a substitute for your judgment, and it is not a mind you can ask, “Give me a ₹20-crore business idea.”

A weak prompt is “My target audience for a healthy snack.” The model will agree with you and hand back “everyone.”

A strong prompt gives product facts, geography, price, and the job you want done:

```text
Act as a customer-strategy consultant.
I am launching a ₹60 baked snack with 15 grams of protein,
sold mainly online in metro Indian cities.
Generate 5 plausible customer segments.
For each segment, explain: primary need, purchase trigger, and key objection.
Do not make "everyone who likes namkeen" a segment.
```

Then **stress-test** the value proposition and positioning you wrote. AI is trained to agree. Ask it not to.

```text
Act as my toughest competitor, then as a sceptical customer
from the office-professional segment.
Here is my positioning statement:
[paste]
1) What is unclear?
2) What is not unique?
3) What would make this customer shrug?
4) Rewrite only if you can keep the same target and the same proof (15g protein, baked, Indian flavour, ₹60).
```

You can also make the model **role-play one persona** — a price-sensitive office employee, or a fitness enthusiast — and ask which messages work, which feel preachy, and which objections appear first.

Treat every AI persona as a **hypothesis**. Validate it with customer conversations, surveys, website analytics, social comments, and — most important — **who actually buys**. If the model said “Bengaluru, age 28, high willingness to pay” and your orders say otherwise, the orders win.

**Activity: Segment, Then Attack Your Own Line**

Run the consultant prompt for FitFataak or your product. Pick one segment. Paste your positioning into the competitor/sceptic prompt. Change the line only where the critique is specific.

## Strategy Becomes Words: Why a Brief Beats a Wish

You now know the customer. The next problem is **what you say**.

“Empowering tomorrow through next-gen customer-centric innovation” helps nobody. If the 4 p.m. insight does not appear in the words, the whole STP exercise was decoration.

Copy has a job. It must **get attention**, create a little **curiosity**, state the **value**, **reduce an objection**, **build trust**, and end with a **call to action (CTA)** — the one thing the person should do next.

Your STP output is the **input** to AI. Think of the model as a **junior copywriter**. With no brief, you get “Fuel your life” — a line that could sell protein powder, petrol, shoes, an energy drink, or a dating app.

Give six inputs every time:

| Brief field | FitFataak example |
|-------------|-------------------|
| Audience | Busy working professionals |
| Problem | 4 p.m. hunger plus an unhealthy desk snack |
| Promise / outcome | Tasty, protein-rich evening snacking |
| Proof | 15 grams of protein; baked; Indian flavours |
| Tone | Witty and relatable — not a preachy fitness coach |
| Action | Try / buy FitFataak |

Fevicol ads work because the **tone** is locked (that long 1990s visual joke). Zomato and Airtel ads work when the voice matches the positioning. Tone is not decoration; it is strategy made audible.

A weak tagline prompt is “Write a tagline.” A stronger one names length, forbidden generic words, and the brief above.

Lines written from a full brief look like this:

- Headline: “4 p.m. called. It wants better snacks.” Body: skip the oily samosa and the boring biscuit; grab FitFataak with 15 grams of protein and flavours made for Indian cravings.
- “Your 4 p.m. meeting is painful enough. Your snack shouldn’t be.” Then: crunch without the guilt.

Those lines name the **occasion**, the **enemy snack**, and the **proof**.

### Taglines, Headlines, and Why They Are Not the Same

- **Official Definition:** A **tagline** is a long-memory brand line. A **landing-page headline** is a high-clarity line that must say what you sell, who it is for, and why it is different — because search and the first screen punish fog.
- **In Simple Words:** A tagline can be clever. A landing-page headline must be obvious.
- **Real-Life Example:** A GPS product sold to US truck owners used a panic subject line: “Your truck is stopped in the Bay Area by federal cops.” Open rates rose. The body then had to deliver the real offer — live visibility — so curiosity opened the mail and clarity closed it.

| Line | Job it does well | Job it may miss |
|------|------------------|-----------------|
| Protein meets namkeen | Awareness and curiosity | May not sell the 4 p.m. occasion |
| Beat the 4 p.m. hunger | Owns the occasion; useful in paid social | Less of a long-term brand chant |
| Your chai deserves better | Relatable at tea time | Weaker if the buyer is not a chai person |

Fitkuh-style ads show the same split in the market: celebrity and product-feature films for **awareness** and **proof**, then an offer film for **CTA**. One line cannot do every job.

Creative angles you can ask AI for, in one prompt:

| Angle | FitFataak flavour |
|-------|-------------------|
| Functional | 15 grams of protein for 4 p.m. hunger |
| Emotional | A snack without guilt |
| Occasion-led | The gap between lunch and dinner |
| Behavioural | Skip the samosa |
| Humorous | Your 4 p.m. hunger has no HR policy |

Weak landing headline: “Redefining the future of healthy snacking.” It does not say what you sell, who it is for, what is different, or what to do.

Strong landing headline: “The protein-rich Indian snack built for 4 p.m. hunger.” Supporting line: baked snacks with 15 grams of protein and familiar Indian flavour, made for busy workdays.

**Activity: Six-Input Brief, Then Six Angles**

Paste the six-field brief into your AI tool. Ask for two taglines, two landing-page headlines, and two short value lines. Demand one benefit-led, one problem-led, one humorous, one premium, one direct, and one witty option. Ban “fuel your life,” “reimagining,” and “next-gen.”

## Evaluate, Refine, Humanise

The first draft is **raw material**. Do not ship it.

Work in four steps: **generate**, **evaluate**, **refine**, **humanise**. Humanising matters because a lot of AI copy leans on em-dashes, “not just this, but that,” and oversized words. Write yourself a standing prompt that forbids those habits, shortens sentences, and sounds like a witty colleague — not a nutrition coach or a consulting slide.

### Three Filters: Tone, Clarity, Resonance

| Filter | Question | Fail example | Pass example |
|--------|----------|--------------|--------------|
| Tone | Does this sound like us? | A premium home-decor brand suddenly sounding like a meme page | Witty desk-snack energy, not a lecture |
| Clarity | Is it obvious in one read? | “Scientifically optimised nutritional snacking ecosystem” | “Protein-rich snack for busy workdays” |
| Resonance | Does it step into their afternoon? | “Our snack contains 15 grams of protein” (clear, cold) | “Stay full through the last two meetings of the day” |

Resonance is harder than clarity. Clarity reports a fact; resonance **mirrors the customer’s situation**. That is **empathy** — standing in their shoes — and the opposite is **apathy**: copy that could sit on any pack in any aisle.

**The one-breath test:** read the line aloud. If you cannot understand it in one breath, it is too complex.

Then make AI a **sceptical senior copywriter** who wants to find faults:

```text
Act as a sceptical senior copywriter who dislikes vague work.
Audience: [paste]
Positioning: [paste]
Copy to judge: [paste]
Score tone, clarity, and resonance (1–5).
List the questions a busy professional would ask.
Do not rewrite until I ask. Find the faults first.
```

Never ask “Is this good copy?” Good is subjective. Ask diagnostic questions: Is it **clear** on first read? **Specific** to us? **Relevant** to this buyer? **Distinctive** if you hide the brand name? **Believable**? Will it **drive an action**?

### Why Human Judgment Still Wins

AI does not carry your **brand history**, local nuance, competitive context, double-meaning risk, or the values the company will not break.

Amul began as a farmers’ cooperative in Anand, Gujarat, associated with Verghese Kurien. The Amul girl is usually witty. She was drawn crying the day Kurien died. That kind of cultural memory will not appear in a generic “witty snack” prompt.

If your research says the audience wants healthier food and **actively dislikes hardcore gym culture**, then “No excuses. Just protein.” is the wrong line even if it is punchy. It lectures the very people you claimed to understand.

A useful human-plus-AI loop:

1. You lock strategy (segment, pain, positioning, tone).
2. AI generates options.
3. You (or AI-as-critic) evaluate against the three filters.
4. You refine and cut words.
5. You choose what is worth saying.

![Human-plus-AI copy loop — generate options, evaluate tone clarity and resonance, refine extra words, then humanise what you ship](https://s13n-curr-images-bucket.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/sal-2026/nontech-pm-dm/session05/s05-05-copy-refine-loop.png?v=20261005)

| AI first draft | Human edit |
|---------------|------------|
| Discover our exciting range of delicious and nutritionally balanced protein-rich snacks specially designed for today’s modern busy professionals | Protein-rich snack for busy workdays |
| Unlock seamless nutrition for modern living | Stay full through the last two meetings |
| Empowering you to live better | Your chai deserves a better 4 p.m. |

If you hide the brand name and the line could sell a bank, an insurer, or a hospital, it is not category-specific enough. “Your TV deserves the best set-top box” survives that test. “Empowering you to live better” does not.

A strong small ad still does three jobs:

1. **Open a loop:** “Still hungry at 4 p.m.?” — a scroller who lives that hour stops.
2. **Answer with product and difference:** 15 grams of protein, crunch, Indian flavour you actually enjoy.
3. **Ask for a low-commitment action:** “Try FitFataak.” Not “Learn more.” Not “Explore.”

Distribution can hide weak positioning for a while. **Pulse** candy scaled partly because the DS Group already owned **Rajnigandha**. Many kirana and pan shops could not afford to drop that hero product, so they also had to stock Pulse.

Very few companies in India have that kind of distribution muscle. Most of us have to win with **STP**, a real **point of difference**, and words that match the customer we chose.

**Activity: One-Breath, Competitor Test, Human Edit**

Take your favourite AI headline. Apply the one-breath test. Hide the brand name. If it could sell insurance, rewrite. Then cut it to the fewest words that still name the occasion and the proof.

## Key Takeaways

- Marketing starts with a **choice**: who you will serve, and who you will ignore. **STP** is how you make that choice visible — divide, pick, then own one idea.
- **Demographics** tell you who they are. **Behaviour and psychographics** tell you why they buy. A census twin like Aman versus Karan is not one customer.
- A **target audience** is a group. A **persona** (Hungry Harsh) adds goal, pain, objection, and trigger so you know what to say at 4 p.m.
- Customers compare you with substitutes, not only twins. Hold **points of parity**. Win with a **point of difference** that is relevant, hard to copy, and deliverable. Features must pass **So What?** until they become value.
- AI can hypothesise segments and draft copy. It will agree with a lazy brief. Feed it STP, then **validate** people in the real world and **humanise** every line for tone, clarity, and resonance.

Upcoming work takes the segment and positioning you locked here and turns them into **channels**, a one-page go-to-market snapshot, and the difference between a launch plan and everyday marketing.

## Important Commands, Libraries & Terminologies

| Term | What it means |
|------|----------------|
| **Consumer vs customer** | Who uses the product versus who pays for it |
| **STP** | Segmentation, targeting, positioning |
| **Segmentation** | Dividing a market into groups with meaningfully different needs or behaviour |
| **Demographic / geographic / psychographic / behavioural** | Who they are; where they are; how they think; what they do |
| **Good-segment tests** | Measurable, substantial, reachable, differentiable, actionable |
| **Targeting scorecard** | Scoring segments on size, problem, willingness to pay, reach, crowding, brand fit |
| **Target audience** | The group you choose to serve |
| **Buyer persona** | A named picture with goal, pain, objection, and trigger |
| **Point of parity (POP)** | The must-haves that keep you in the category |
| **Point of difference (POD)** | The specific reason to choose you |
| **Value proposition** | For [audience] who [problem], [product] provides [benefit] because [proof] |
| **Positioning statement** | For [market] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit] |
| **Positioning vs tagline** | Internal idea you own versus the creative public line |
| **So What? test** | Keep asking until a feature becomes a customer outcome |
| **Feature / benefit / value** | Spec → what it lets me do → why my life is better |
| **Creative brief (six inputs)** | Audience, problem, promise, proof, tone, action |
| **Tagline vs landing-page headline** | Memory line versus high-clarity first screen |
| **CTA** | The one next action you want (try, buy) — not “learn more” by default |
| **Tone / clarity / resonance** | Sounds like us; understood in one read; mirrors their situation |
| **One-breath test** | If you cannot get the meaning in one breath, the copy is too heavy |
| **Competitor / brand-swap test** | Hide the brand; if another category could run the line, rewrite |
| **Humanise** | Strip AI habits (em-dashes, “not just… but…”, jargon) and keep brand judgment |
